DewLab · eleven animated openers · six scenes each, about seventy seconds

How to use this in Canva. Landscape Video, 1920×1080. One Canva page per scene — six pages. Put the guide's picture on every page so they are on screen throughout. The caption is the on-screen text; the narration is what gets spoken, either recorded by Ms. Nia or by Canva’s text to speech.

Export as MP4, upload to YouTube as Unlisted, then paste the link in the admin panel under Record a session.

What Grows When

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1Cool blue sky. Small pale sun high right. Thermometer on the left, blue and low. A full round lettuce on the left of the soil; on the right, just a seed sitting in the earth. Month chip: FEBRUARY.Every seed has weather it likes.Hi, I am Root. Today we are not asking what to plant. We are asking when. Every seed has weather it likes.
2Same scene, hold. Push in slightly on the lettuce so its leaves fill more of the frame.Lettuce likes it cool.This is lettuce. Lettuce likes it cool. In February it is happy — crisp, sweet, ready to pick.
3Sky warms toward cream. Sun grows and shifts higher. Thermometer climbing. Lettuce starting to look leggy; the okra seed has sprouted two small leaves. Month chip: MAY.But watch what happens in July…But watch what happens when the months go by, and Texas turns the heat up.
4Sky is warm orange. Sun large and deep orange. Thermometer red and high. THE LETTUCE HAS BOLTED — a tall bare stalk shooting up with a small flower on top. Month chip: JULY.It shoots up tall and turns bitter. That is bolting.The lettuce shoots up tall and goes bitter. Gardeners call that bolting. It is not your fault. It is the wrong month.
5Same July scene. Now push in on the okra: tall, broad leaves, two green pods. It is thriving while the lettuce behind it has gone.Okra is the opposite. It loves this.Now look at the okra. Same garden. Same water. This one loves the heat, and it is finally taking off.
6Sky cools back to blue. Sun small again. A fresh round lettuce is back; the okra is a seed again. Month chip: OCTOBER. Ends where it began.Same garden. Different month.Same garden, same water, different month. So before you plant, ask what weather this seed wants. Get the month right, and the garden does the rest.

Who Moves the Pollen

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1Two pink flowers on a soft blue-green background, a little apart from each other. Nothing else. Still and waiting.Every flower is waiting for a visitor.Hi, I am Buzz. Every flower you see is waiting for a visitor. It cannot walk to another flower, so it needs someone to come to it.
2A bee flies in and lands on the left flower.Bees come for the nectar. Not for us.Bees come for the nectar — the sweet drink inside. They are not doing us a favour. They are getting their dinner.
3Close on the bee. Small yellow dots of pollen appear stuck to its back and legs.Pollen sticks to them without them noticing.While they drink, yellow pollen sticks all over them. They do not even notice they are carrying it.
4The bee flies to the right flower. A dotted yellow trail arcs between the two, and pollen dots land on the second flower.They carry it to the next flower.Then off they go to the next flower, and some of that pollen rubs off. That is the whole trick.
5The left flower is still a flower. The right one has turned into a small orange fruit on a stem.And that is how a fruit starts.Once the pollen arrives, a seed can start to grow, and a fruit grows around it. No visit, no fruit.
6Both are now fruit. The bee hovers between them. Pull back to show the whole pair.No pollinators, no apples. It is that direct.So when we plant flowers for bees and leave the spray in the shed, we are not being kind. We are keeping our own dinner growing.

Everybody Needs a Home

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A simple house icon, centred, on a soft blue-green background.Every animal needs four things.Hi, I am Buzz. Every animal alive needs the same four things. Not three. Four.
2Four icons appear one at a time in a square: an apple, a water drop, a house, a map.Food. Water. Shelter. Space.Food to eat. Water to drink. Shelter to hide in. And space — room enough to find it all.
3A green patch of ground fills the middle. A frog, a bee and a bird appear on it, spaced out.A place with all four is a habitat.A place that has all four is called a habitat. Not a house. A habitat can be a pond, a log pile, or one good hedge.
4The green patch fades to dull grey-green. The water drop above it gets a red cross through it. The animals fade until only one faint bee is left.Take one away, and it stops working.Now watch. Take away just the water, and it does not matter how good the rest is. They have to leave.
5Everything clears. One large water drop, centred, on its own.Most of the time, the missing one is water.And here is the useful bit. In most gardens, the one that is missing is water — which happens to be the easiest of the four to add.
6The green patch returns, full colour. Frog, bee and butterfly are back, and a water drop sits above them.A dish of water is a habitat you built.A shallow dish with stones in it. That is all. Put it out, and something will find it. Then you have not just learned about a habitat. You made one.

What Compost Eats

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A single banana peel, large, centred, on a warm cream background.This is a banana peel. Watch it.Hi, I am Wiggles. This is a banana peel. Most people call it rubbish. Give me twelve weeks and I will show you what it really is.
2The peel is smaller now. Tiny microbe shapes appear either side of it and a worm below.Something alive comes to eat it.The moment it lands, things arrive. Bacteria you cannot see. Fungi. Then bugs, and then me.
3More microbes. A thermometer appears at the right, rising. The peel is visibly smaller.They are eating. That is all decomposing is.Decomposing sounds complicated. It is not. It is a lot of small things eating, and the pile gets warm because of it.
4Three things side by side: a lettuce leaf (small), a dry leaf (medium), a woody twig (large and intact). Size shows how long each takes.Soft things go first. Woody things take months.The soft wet things vanish in days. A dry twig can still be there months later. That is why gardeners mix wet greens with dry browns.
5A dark brown band of soil across the middle. A crumbly handful sits on it.And what is left is soil.What you are left with is dark, crumbly, and smells like a forest floor. That is compost, and plants grow better in it than in anything you can buy.
6The soil band with a tomato growing out of it, and a recycling arrow above.Rubbish in one end. Soil out the other.So nothing was thrown away. It went round. That peel is going to be a tomato, and it never left your garden.

Under Your Feet

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1Four horizontal bands stacked: light brown, dark brown, mid brown, grey. Seen side-on, like a cut through the ground.Soil is not dirt. It has layers.Hi, I am Wiggles. People say dirt like it is one thing. It is not. Dig down and it changes, over and over.
2Same four bands. A leaf sits on the very top band, and a small label points at it reading 'this year'.The top is only this year.The very top is just this year. Leaves, twigs, whatever fell. Come back in a season and it will have joined the layer below.
3The second band darkens. A worm and a small plant root appear inside it.Under it: topsoil. Everything grows here.Under that is topsoil. Dark, crumbly and completely alive. Almost every root you have ever seen was in this one layer.
4Same bands. Large white text across the topsoil band: 500 YEARS.An inch of it takes about five hundred years.And here is the part that stops people. One inch of topsoil takes something like five hundred years to make. One inch.
5The bands are thinner and messier. Water drops fall from above and the top bands are visibly washing away.Rain can take it away in an afternoon.And if the ground is bare, a single heavy rain can wash it down the hill in an afternoon. Five hundred years, gone in one storm.
6The bands are restored, and the top band is green with grass. Small plants sit on top holding it in place.Which is why we cover it.That is the whole reason gardeners mulch, and plant cover crops, and hate bare ground. Roots hold it. Nothing else does.

Build a Plate

1An empty white plate, centred, on a warm cream background.Your plate is a picture of your afternoon.Hi, I am Chef Sprout. What goes on your plate decides how you feel at four o clock. Not next year. This afternoon.
2Five food icons in a row above the plate: apple, leafy green, bread, meat, milk.There are five groups. Only five.There are five food groups. Fruit. Vegetables. Grains. Protein. And dairy. That is the whole list.
3The plate with its left half shaded green. A salad and an apple sit in that half.Half the plate is fruit and vegetables.And here is the only rule you need to remember. Half the plate. Fruit and vegetables. Half.
4The same plate, now with the right half shaded tan too. Bread and chicken in that half. All four quadrants filled.The other half does the rest of the job.The other half is grains and protein. They are not the enemy. They are just not half.
5The plate with a single biscuit on it. Nothing else. Not a scolding — just a fact.A treat is not a mistake.And a biscuit is not a crime. Most days need fuel. Some days have a biscuit in them too. Both things are true.
6A realistic everyday plate: carrots on the green half, rice and chicken on the other.Draw the meal you really ate. Then add one thing.So do not aim for a perfect plate. Draw the one you actually ate this week, and add one fruit or one vegetable next time. One.

How Food Gets To You

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1One large tomato, centred, on a warm neutral background.Somebody grew this. Somebody picked it.Hi, I am Chef Sprout. Pick up any food in your kitchen. Somebody grew that. Somebody picked it, by hand, on a particular morning.
2Three icons in a row: a picking basket, a box, a snowflake.Then it gets packed and cooled.Then it gets packed, and cooled, because the clock starts the moment it leaves the plant.
3Three icons in a row: a lorry, a ship, a plane.Then it travels. Sometimes very far.Then it travels. A tomato from a Texas farm might drive for two hours. A banana spends two weeks on a ship.
4Two tomatoes side by side. The left one pale green, labelled 'picked green'. The right one deep red, labelled 'picked ripe'.That is why some fruit is picked green.And that is the bit worth knowing. Fruit that has to travel gets picked before it is ripe, so it survives the trip. That is why a shop tomato tastes different from yours.
5A bin, centred and large. Small icons around it: a water drop, a lorry, a clock — everything that gets wasted with it.Every step costs something.Every step costs fuel, and water, and somebody’s work. Which is why throwing food away wastes far more than the food.
6Three things in a row: a seedling, an arrow, a plate. The shortest journey there is.The shortest journey is from your own garden.And the shortest journey in the world is from your garden to your plate. No lorry, no ship, no picking it early. Three metres.

Design a Seed Traveller

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A tree, and one seed falling directly below it. Soft blue-green background.A seed has a problem. It cannot walk.Hi, I am Tinker. Every seed has the same problem, and it is a good one. It has to get away from its parent, and it cannot walk.
2The same tree with three crowded tiny seedlings squeezed underneath it, all competing.Drop straight down and you fight your own parent.If it just drops, it lands in its parent’s shadow and fights it for light and water. It will probably lose.
3Three seeds side by side: a dandelion clock, a burr, a coconut.So seeds are engineered to travel.So seeds are built to move. And every one of them is a solved engineering problem you can copy.
4Four icons in a row: wind, a dog, water, an explosion. The four ways seeds travel.Wind. Animals. Water. Some just explode.A dandelion catches the wind. A burr hooks onto a dog. A coconut floats. And some pods just dry out and fire the seeds across the garden.
5Three icons: a tool, a ruler, a circular arrow. Build, measure, change one thing.Engineers do the same thing: try, measure, change one thing.When you build your own, do what engineers do. Try it. Measure it. Change one thing. Try again. Not two things. One.
6A single book icon, centred. A worse result is still an answer.A worse result is still an answer.And if it flies worse, that is not a failure. That is you finding out something that does not work, which is exactly as useful.

Catching the Sun

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A large orange sun with a beam of light coming down from it. Warm cream sky.Sunlight is not just light. It is energy.Hi, I am Tinker. When sunlight lands on something, it does not just light it up. It heats it. That is energy arriving.
2Two squares side by side: one black, one white. Labelled 'hot' and 'cool' underneath.Dark things soak it up. Light things bounce it.Dark things soak that energy up and get hot. Light and shiny things bounce it away and stay cool. That is the whole trick.
3A sun in the top right with a dotted light beam bouncing off a grey foil panel and down to the left.Foil lets you aim it.Which means foil is useful. It does not absorb the sunlight, it bounces it, so you can point it wherever you want.
4A pale blue box seen side-on, with a light ray entering through the top and a thermometer inside it.Glass lets light in and traps the heat.And clear plastic or glass does something clever. Light goes straight through it, but once that light has become heat, it cannot get back out.
5A pizza box oven seen side-on: tan box, black inside, sun above, a snack sitting in it.Put all three together and you have an oven.Black to absorb, foil to aim, and a lid to trap. Put all three in a pizza box and you have built something that cooks with no fuel at all.
6The same oven, but the inside is pale not black, and there is a red cross on the snack. Take a part away and it stops working.Take one away and it barely warms up.And here is your experiment. Take any one of the three away, and it hardly warms at all. Try it, and you will know exactly what each part was for.

Drying the Harvest

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A single fresh green leaf, large and centred. Two small microbe shapes either side of it.Food does not just go bad on its own.Hi, I am Mason. Food does not rot because time passed. It rots because something moved in and started eating it.
2Three icons in a row: a thermometer, food, a water drop. The three things spoiling needs.Mould and bacteria need three things.Mould and bacteria need three things to grow. Warmth. Food. And water. Take away any one of them and they stop.
3One large water drop, centred, with a red cross over it.Warmth and food are hard to remove. Water is not.You cannot take the food away, obviously. And keeping everything cold takes a fridge. But the water? The water you can just let leave.
4Three leaves hanging upside down from a horizontal line, drying.That is all drying is.So you hang your herbs somewhere warm and airy, and the water walks out into the air. Nothing left to drink. Nothing can grow.
5Two leaves side by side. Left labelled 'crumbles', right labelled 'bends'. One is dry and papery, one still soft.Dry enough means it crumbles, not bends.And here is how you know it is done. A dry leaf crumbles. A leaf that bends is still holding water, and water in the jar is where mould starts.
6A sealed jar, centred, with a warning triangle above it. This is the one to ask a grown-up about.Canning is different. That one needs a grown-up.One last thing, and I mean it. Drying herbs is safe to do yourself. Putting food in sealed jars is not. That needs a grown-up and tested instructions, every single time.

Saving Seeds for Next Year

Six scenes · about 70 seconds · guide picture: see the file names sent with this

1A single seed, large and centred, with a small sleep symbol above it.A seed is alive. It is just asleep.Hi, I am Mason. A seed is not a thing. It is a plant, alive, and asleep. Everything it needs to wake up is already packed inside.
2Two plants side by side. The left one tall and healthy, labelled 'this one'. The right one small and wilting.Save from your best plant, not your worst.So take your seed from the best plant you grew. The one that did well here, in your soil, in your heat. Those are the traits you are keeping.
3Two pods side by side. Left brown and dry, labelled 'ready'. Right green and full, labelled 'not yet'.Wait until the plant says it is finished.And wait. A brown rattling pod. A sunflower head that has drooped. The plant tells you when the seed is finished. Take it early and it will not grow.
4Four seeds spread in a single row on a flat surface, drying. Not in a heap.Then dry it properly. Really properly.Spread them out somewhere airy for a week. Press a fingernail in. If it dents, it is not dry, and a damp seed in a sealed jar turns to mould.
5A paper envelope, centred, with SUNFLOWER written on it and 'saved this year' underneath.Cool, dark, dry — and write the year on it.Then a paper envelope, somewhere cool and dark. Write what it is and the year, because seeds get less reliable as they age and next spring you will not remember.
6Three things in a row: a bowl of seeds, an arrow, a seedling. The loop closing.And next spring, you do not buy anything.And that is the whole point. Next spring you open a drawer instead of a shop, and the garden starts again from a plant you chose yourself.
